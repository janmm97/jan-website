VA tasks AI can already do (and the ones it can't)

November 5, 20268 min read1784 words

AI can already handle the repeatable parts of a virtual assistant's week: sorting an inbox, drafting a summary, moving data between apps and offering meeting slots. It can't own a client relationship, judge a delicate situation or answer for a result. The useful split is by risk. Work that is cheap to get wrong can run with light checking. Work that touches money, the calendar, sending or a client's trust should wait for a person's yes.

Key takeaways

  • Sort a VA's tasks into three groups: AI runs them, AI drafts and a person approves, or a person keeps them.
  • Anything that sends, spends, deletes, shares or changes the calendar waits for a human yes.
  • Relationships, judgement on edge cases and accountability stay human.
  • You can sort your own VA hours in one week, using the method below.

What does a virtual assistant do in a week?

CareerExplorer's virtual assistant page is the base for the task list. It says VAs "manage emails, calendars, appointments, and phone calls," and that some "create and schedule posts, respond to comments, and help grow the client's online presence" (CareerExplorer). The same page lists "data entry, preparing reports, and maintaining records" as common duties. Those duties vary by client. The list below is the base I use for the sort in the rest of this post.

  • Inbox: triage, replies, follow-ups
  • Calendar: bookings, reminders, rescheduling
  • Documents: data entry, reports, records
  • Communication: customer requests, basic support
  • Content: posts, newsletters, comment replies
  • Admin: invoices, bookkeeping support, research

The page doesn't give a date, and it doesn't say how much of this time AI can absorb. The sort below is my judgement, not a figure from a study.

Which VA tasks can AI run on its own?

These tasks follow clear rules, and a wrong result costs little. A tool can run them with light checking. Each one still needs a rule for what the tool may see and when a person looks at the output.

  • Inbox sorting: label, archive and flag messages by sender or topic. Check the rules once a week, because a bad rule can hide a client's reply.
  • Meeting notes: turn a transcript into a summary and a list of action items. Check the names and the dates before the summary goes anywhere.
  • Data entry: copy details from a form or an email into a spreadsheet. Spot-check a sample each day, since a shifted column can ruin a whole batch.
  • Scheduling suggestions: list open slots from a calendar. Nothing is booked until a person confirms.
  • Research gathering: collect links and quotes for a person to read. Treat every fact as unchecked until someone opens the source.

Which VA tasks should AI draft while a person approves?

These tasks need a human decision before anything leaves the building. AI saves the typing. The person keeps the judgement and the send button.

  • Client emails and replies: the tool writes the first version. The VA reads it, fixes the tone and sends it.
  • Social posts: the tool drafts options. A person picks one and checks every fact in it.
  • Invoices and payment reminders: the tool prepares them. A person confirms the amount and the recipient.
  • Reports: the tool pulls the figures together. A person checks the numbers and writes the takeaway.
  • Calendar changes with other people: the tool proposes a time. A person confirms it.

My rule: anything involving money, sending, deleting or sharing waits for a human yes. The approval step is the point of the setup, not an extra. Without it, a fast draft becomes a wrong invoice in a client's inbox. Here is one invoice reminder from start to finish. The tool pulls the invoice number, the due date and the number of days overdue, then drafts a reminder. The VA checks the amount against the contract, confirms the client's address and sends it. If the amount is wrong, the reminder never leaves the drafts folder. That single check is the whole value of the approval step.

Which VA tasks are still human?

Three kinds of work stay with a person, however good the tools get.

  • Relationships: a client who trusts a VA with their business is trusting a person. A tool can write the warm reply. It can't be the one the client calls when something goes wrong.
  • Judgement on edge cases: a client asks for a change that conflicts with an earlier instruction. A person asks the question that resolves it. A tool often guesses.
  • Accountability: when a task goes wrong, the client wants one person who owns the fix. That owner is a person.

A late payment is a good test. The tool can list the overdue invoices and draft the reminder. A person decides whether to send it, whether to call first and what to say if the client is upset.

What are the risks?

The risks are specific, and most come from one step: letting a tool act without a check.

  • Wrong recipient: an email goes to the wrong person. Keep send rights with the VA, and check the address on every outbound message.
  • Made-up details: a summary states a decision nobody made. Check names, numbers and dates against the source.
  • Access and privacy: a tool with full inbox access can see everything in it. Give it the narrowest access that does the job, and remove access when the client leaves.

Here is a made-up details example. A tool summarises a call and writes that the client agreed to a Friday deadline. The call notes only say "maybe Friday." The summary is fluent and wrong, and it reads as if a person wrote it. The check is to compare every date and commitment with the source line before the summary is filed or sent. I haven't found a source for how often these failures happen, so this post doesn't give a rate.

How do I sort my VA hours in one week?

Here is the method, in six steps. It takes one week and a notebook.

  1. Log every task for five working days. Write the task, the minutes it took and whether it touched a client.
  2. Sort each line into one of the three groups: AI runs it, AI drafts and a person approves, or a person keeps it.
  3. Pick one task in the first group that repeats every week. It should follow the same steps each time.
  4. Write the steps down. Add one approval step before anything reaches a customer.
  5. Run that one task with the new step for two weeks. Watch the first ten runs by hand.
  6. Review the log. If the task still needs fixing every time, move it back to the drafting group.

You don't need a number to make this useful. The log tells you which hours are repeatable, and that's enough to decide what to try next.

What does this mean for VAs?

The tasks AI takes first are the ones you did on autopilot. Your value moves toward the third group: judgement, communication and ownership. A VA who can set up a tool, check its output and explain it to a client keeps a role that a tool can't fill alone. Learn to build the automations you used to do by hand. Start with one tool and one repeatable task this month. The next step is running and checking automations, which I cover in the advanced AI skills post, once it is live. Frequently asked questions

What are the typical tasks a virtual assistant performs?

CareerExplorer describes VAs managing emails, calendars, appointments and phone calls, and creating and scheduling posts. It also lists data entry, preparing reports and maintaining records as common parts of the role (CareerExplorer). The list varies by client, so start with your own week.

What tasks can an AI assistant do?

The tasks I would hand to a tool on its own are the rule-based ones: inbox sorting, meeting summaries, data entry and research gathering. Tasks with money, sending or client-facing words run best as drafts with an approval step. These are my observations, not a benchmark.

What does an AI virtual assistant do?

It is software that handles a set of VA tasks. The useful part is the approval step: the tool drafts or prepares, and a person decides what goes out. The sources I checked don't define the term, so I use it only in this sense.

Will AI replace virtual assistants?

I haven't found a source that measures how many VA jobs AI will change, so this post gives no figure. The work that is most repeatable moves first. Work that depends on relationships, judgement and ownership stays with people.

Is it safe to give AI access to my inbox?

Give it the narrowest access that does the job, and check the rules it applies. Keep send rights with a person, and remove access when a client leaves. These are my practice rules. They are not a security audit, and your provider's settings and your client's data policy come first.
