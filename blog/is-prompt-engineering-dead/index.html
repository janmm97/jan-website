Is Prompt Engineering Dead?

November 10, 20269 min read1830 words

Mostly, yes. Prompt engineering as its own skill, the clever wording and tricks people traded in 2023 and 2024, has had its day. What took its place looks more like managing staff: you give an AI agent a goal, the right context and examples of good work, then you check what it hands back. Anthropic calls the wider job context engineering.

Key takeaways

  • OpenAI's own guide says reasoning models do better with a goal than with a script, so the wording tricks count for less.
  • Anthropic calls context engineering "the natural progression of prompt engineering." The job now covers everything the model sees.
  • Nick Saraev says it outright in his video: prompt engineering "is not the skill that matters anymore," and he manages AI agents like staff instead.
  • What lasted looks like delegation: a goal, context, examples, feedback and tests.
  • In my view, "prompt engineer" as a job title got more hype than work.

Why do people say prompt engineering is dead?

Three reasons come up, and the first two come from the model makers' own docs.

Plain instructions work better now. OpenAI's prompt engineering guide says reasoning models "will provide better results on tasks with only high-level guidance." It contrasts them with GPT models, which "benefit from very precise instructions." Its analogy: "A reasoning model is like a senior co-worker. You can give them a goal to achieve and trust them to work out the details."

The prompt is now a small part of the input. Anthropic says agents need strategies "for managing the entire context state (system instructions, tools, Model Context Protocol (MCP), external data, message history, etc)." Once an agent runs in a loop, the prompt you wrote is one piece of that.

AI went from tool to staff. Nick Saraev makes the practical case in "Is Prompt Engineering Still Worth It in 2027?". His summary of the video: "Prompt engineering is not the skill that matters anymore. Here's how I manage AI agents like staff instead: clear goals, good context, examples, training loops, regular evals and cost control."

I agree with him. When I hand work to an agent, the goal, the material and the check I give it decide the result far more than how I phrase the request.

What is context engineering?

Anthropic draws the line this way. Prompt engineering "refers to methods for writing and organizing LLM instructions for optimal outcomes." Context engineering "refers to the set of strategies for curating and maintaining the optimal set of tokens (information) during LLM inference, including all the other information that may land there outside of the prompts."

The problem is what happens as the window fills. Anthropic points to Chroma's research on "context rot": as the number of tokens grows, the model gets less accurate at recalling information from them. Anthropic also describes an "attention budget." Every new token uses some of it, so the goal is "the smallest possible set of high-signal tokens that maximize the likelihood of some desired outcome."

Anthropic describes ways to deal with the limit. One is sub-agents: "specialized sub-agents can handle focused tasks with clean context windows," while the lead agent keeps the plan and works with what they return. Another is compaction, which Anthropic mentions for long-horizon tasks.

What replaced it: managing AI like staff

Saraev's video lays out how he runs agents, and the chapter list reads like an onboarding plan for a new employee:

  1. Treat the AI like a new employee.
  2. Set SMART goals.
  3. Give it context with a knowledge base.
  4. Show it examples of good work.
  5. Train it on real work.
  6. Benchmark every new model.
  7. Track what agents cost, and cut it.

Only one of those steps touches wording, and it is about examples. The other six are management.

My version: a prompt is a delegation brief

I work the same way. When I hand a task to an agent, I write it the way I'd brief a VA. Each part of the brief maps to a line in the prompt:

  • The goal and why it matters: the reason the task exists.
  • What done looks like: the check the output has to pass.
  • The materials: the files, data or examples the model needs.
  • One example: the shape you want back.
  • What not to do: the limits, like no deleting and no sending.
  • When to stop and ask: the cases where the model should check with you.

The pair below is for a made-up task: sorting a week's inbox.

Before: "Sort my inbox."

After: "You're helping me clear my inbox for the week. Goal: anything urgent gets a reply today, anything else gets a reply this week, and newsletters get archived. Done means every email sits in one of those three buckets, with a one-line reason for each reply-today email. Materials: the list below (sender, subject, first two lines). Example: 'Invoice #123 due Friday' goes in reply today, because the reason is 'payment due Friday'. Don't draft replies and don't delete anything. If an email could be a client or a payment, stop and ask me."

The second version has no clever wording in it. It reads like a note to a new assistant on their first day. I check the result against the "done" line before I look at anything else. If an email lands in the wrong bucket, the brief was vague, so I fix the brief and rerun.

Is prompt engineering still worth learning?

As a skill on its own, I wouldn't spend months on it. Bernard Marr argued in Forbes in January 2026 that prompt engineering isn't the most valuable AI skill in 2026. One line from his piece sums up the shift: "prompting, though still vital, takes a back seat to the ability to exercise judgement over when, where and how to use AI."

Spend the time on delegation instead: setting goals, picking the right material, writing examples and reviewing output. Those skills still work when the model changes, and you'll pick up the prompting along the way.

How I test an agent before I trust it

Anthropic's prompt engineering overview asks for three things before you start: clear success criteria, some ways to test against them, and a first draft prompt. OpenAI advises pinning production apps to a specific model snapshot, so the behaviour doesn't change under you. This is the method I use:

  1. Write down what done looks like.
  2. Collect 10 real inputs.
  3. Run them and mark each one pass or fail.
  4. Change one thing at a time.
  5. Pin the model version.
  6. Re-test when a new model comes out.
  7. Check what a run costs before you scale it up.

I change one thing at a time because otherwise you can't tell which change helped. A brief that passes ten inputs once can still fail on the eleventh, and a model update can break one that worked last month. Keep the ten inputs in one file, so you can rerun the same set after every change. A pass or fail mark is enough at this stage. You don't need a score.

Frequently asked questions

Is prompt engineering dead?

As a standalone skill, mostly yes. The wording tricks faded, and Anthropic now calls the wider job context engineering. What survives of prompt engineering lives inside that job: clear goals, examples and tests.

What has replaced prompt engineering?

Context engineering, in Anthropic's terms, which covers the whole set of tokens the model sees. In day-to-day work it looks like managing staff: you set goals, give context and examples, and review the output. Nick Saraev describes his version as "clear goals, good context, examples, training loops, regular evals and cost control."

Is there any future in prompt engineering?

Not as a title of its own, in my view. Bernard Marr writes in Forbes that prompting "takes a back seat" to judgement about when and how to use AI. The future is in running agents well, and that work still uses what prompting taught us.

Is prompt engineering a good career?

As a job title on its own, I wouldn't bet on it. The skill is worth having inside roles like VA, ops, marketing or development. I haven't seen salary or job-count data from a source I'd trust, so I won't guess.

What is context engineering in AI?

Anthropic defines it as the strategies for curating and maintaining the optimal set of tokens during inference, including information outside the prompt. The aim is the smallest set of high-signal tokens that gets the outcome you want. For a short task that's mostly a good brief. For a long agent run, it's also what you keep in the window and what you leave out.
